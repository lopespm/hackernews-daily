The 'Astra can read and write weights to improve Qwen' post is misleading

"I work in this field (mech interp research) and I agree, the original post is definitely misleading. Some overselling in research is normal but it's a bit much.First, the key result has nothing to do with making Qwen better at math, it just reduces reasoning loops.Reasoning loops are a simple problem: the model generates the </think> token (which closes reasoning) with too low probability. You can fix this with simple steering approaches where you add a vector to different components of the LLM (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22048, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18167, etc). The method Astra used for identifying the vector is the standard procedure humans use (contrastive analysis). Such steering vector creation is basically the standard "intro-level interp project" for human researchers.If you can code and understand LLM internals, you can do this yourself in 4-8 hours. The SWE equivalent would be doing binary patching with a debugger. Cool that frontier models can do this, but not unexpected since humans can do it without issue."

"Speaking for myself. I’ve been aware of a lot of concepts around training, but stayed away from it until very recently.A few weeks ago, I formed a hypothesis that I could get a smaller model good at the specific standards I have for my git commit messages, which are hard to get out of most LLMs today.I spent the last four to five weeks on traditional training. It improved in a lot of areas, but not enough to be usable for me.Yesterday, the article the author of this post is critical of gave me enough of an aha that maybe the problems I was seeing could be solved by having a larger LLM change the weights directly, without going through training.In the last twelve-ish hours I’ve made more headway than I have in 2+ weeks. I’m using one of my last well performing checkpoints (full fine-tune + changes to the decision head/LoRA, etc.) and gave the model free rein to change any of the layers.Its last two actions: (a) removed 16 participant-order directions from a head matrix, which improved one type of reasoning and degraded another, and (b) restored one of the 16, which reversed the degradation while keeping the core improvement.All that to say, this might be known to people who’ve been around training for years, even decades, but it’s new to a lot of us. And we’re excited about it."

Could a modern chess engine on a Pentium have defeated Kasparov in 1996?

"The main point the post is trying to make is accurate, modern chess engines are stronger mostly due to improved eval, search, etc and not hardware; but the idea that Kasparov would lose to one on 90s hardware is not as clearly true as one might think. Modern engine eval functions and search algos make very different depth tradeoffs and when given not enough compute they have glaring weaknesses that Kasparov and his team would likely have exploited.It was trivial for me an expert-ish player (1950 USCF 2300+ bullet) to find a line to beat it. https://nicholas.carlini.com/writing/2026/algorithmic-improv...Also 3000 engine elo is not really a good direct comparison, engine elos are kinda meaningless when comparing human to engines, they are meant to compare engines to engines.A lot of the knowledge tricks that modern engines do require depth or other things that didn't work on older hardware, deep blue was engineered to brute force a lot of things because brute force in the 90s worked better. Historically all sorts of things were tried for chess engines abandoned and tried again. Chess engine dev (and AI in general) is very cyclical; old ideas get returned to and retried as the hardware changes and evolves. Devs from past eras were smarter and more knowledgeable than we give them credit for. Heck, some of the very first engines for checkers and chess were neural nets (of a sort)."
