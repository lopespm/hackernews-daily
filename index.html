Microsoft blames monster email outage on software defect introduced in July fix

"No big surprises here. Big outages at big firms seem to always look like this.The electricity industry has this notion they call a black start, where they bring the grid online from zero. It's a very difficult operation because you can't start a generator straight into an offline grid otherwise it gets hit with full demand and can't keep frequency in the right range, which can damage equipment. So they have to start by disconnecting all the loads and then bringing load blocks back in sequence, trying to roughly balance available generation with the size of the next block. In practice black starts are extremely rare, but they can happen after a cascading failure in which something causes some generating stations to trip, redirecting load onto the remaining stations which can't handle it causing them to trip in turn. The entire grid can collapse in just 60 seconds or so once that kind of runaway failure starts.This kind of outage is very analogous to a black start. A few servers get into trouble - maybe they're in a crash loop or maybe their caches are cold - the load balancers shift traffic onto the remainder which then can't handle the load either and so the failure cascades. Once a service is in this state sometimes the only remedy is to disconnect all load, bring the servers back to stability, then bring load blocks back online in small chunks, making sure to never hit anything with the full load immediately to ensure any caches or JIT compilers have time to warm up.It's the sort of thing that requires a bit of preparation because naive ways of doing it like just randomly dropping 90% of requests will yield a broken service for everyone. Ideally your LB can drop traffic by modulus of the session cookie or similar, so users are let back in atomically.Black starting a service is obviously quite hard to practice, but is probably something that sufficiently well resourced SRE teams should try once a quarter or so on a test cluster. I used to be an SRE at Google and although we regularly started up new clusters and moved traffic around between them, actually starting an isolated service instance entirely from scratch was never something we practiced. I remember in a few cases teams discovered devs had introduced circular dependencies by accident, i.e. starting service X required service X to already exist, or it depended on service Y that in turn depended on X. Sometimes you can only find these things by practicing."

"No one has asked the obvious question: how much AI contributed to the epic failure by Microsoft. Was the patch programmed and tested by AI? Not because they are inherently bad but it begs the question of how much do the remaining humans at MS understand the patch? And did using AI contribute to the atrophy of knowledge and skills, especially deep, specific skills such as network failure inspection, load balancing and fail over recovery, that led to a slower response and recovery time?Without a functioning intuition, discovering the causes to initiate recovery effort in a high-stress, unfamiliar situation with catastrophic dimensions could be significantly hampered.They are valid questions and of particular interest in the age of AI. Productive, highly specialized skills could degrade faster than one imagine once we start rely too much on external helps."
