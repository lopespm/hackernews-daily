USPS now offers official ZIP Code boundary polygons

"Dang, the prices are crazy. $10k for the lowest tier? I expect federal data to be free. Most federal data related to stuff like regulation (e.g. FCC spectrum use coverage maps), maps for federally-managed lands, etc. is free. And, it should be free. It's a core part of accountability and transparency. Historically there was a copying cost (having a human print physical copies on paper or CD/DVD/tape), which is understandable, but now that the incremental cost of each new copy approaches $0, that's what it should cost users of the data."

"I thought these were going to be free and got so excited.FWIW if you're willing to deal with some 'fuzziness' and the occasional missing zip code that's commercial only, the ZCTA shapefiles are free and are close enough for many purposes: https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/geography/guidance/g..."

"This is interesting. For a long time it was pointed out that ZIP codes, unlike say, city or state boundaries, were not properly representable as polygons, since they don't divide up acreage into zones, but rather label postal delivery routes. Suppose I build a new neighborhood way out in the middle of nowhere. The "polygon" concept suggests that we could look and see what polygon I'm in, and that will determine what post office delivers my mail. But in reality, if there was no route to that area already, they could decide to make it part of any one of the surrounding routes.I suppose though, as long as you let the polygons map has uncontained areas, where no routes currently exist, it's a doable task to draw polygons around the limits of every route network, as long as you don't mind that they may have lots of weird ragged edges."

Writing as a thinking tool

"When I had started my PhD, I thought the writing part of a paper was just the last thing I needed to do to summarize my results, like a boring epilogue to the more exciting science. What surprised me was how much of the scientific process relied on my ability to convey my ideas to someone else. And to be able to do that you need clarity of mind, you need to have worked through multiple drafts to be able to get to your point as efficiently and clearly as possible. So many times I only discovered what I was actually researching during the writing part.The problem with AI writing is that it makes people believe that writing is just writing and not a tool for thinking very deeply about the topic you are writing about. I am not completely opposed to AI assisted writing, but if I see AI writing, it’s telling me that the author tried to take a shortcut in their own thinking and it’s a good heuristic that the ideas will not be thought through enough to warrant my complete attention."

"Writing code (Programming) is a thinking tool too.When you are writing code manually, actually you are thinking about what you are doing, what you will build actually, what are your or users' real requirements, etc."

"This is true for designing, too. I see a lot of people saying Figma is dead because you can prototype directly; for novel UX problems the act of designing — of putting ideas together, assembling a component, even getting good data into a design — is where a lot of my thinking and insights develop. The brain work plus the craft work, the tight loop between thinking and creating is so critical for me and I haven’t found nearly the same feeling when prompting.In writing and designing I think we often have a thought-feeling first: something inchoate that we can’t quite articulate yet. Getting directly to the medium itself — text or frames and layouts — is how we give shape to that feeling and turn it into a concept, insight or design."
