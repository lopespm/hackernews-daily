The Goose Programming Language

"Btw I made the language, AMA. repo: https://github.com/aardappel/goose"

"The very first point under your tutorial is factually incorrect. var words: u8[][>..] = []; for i in 5 { words.push(str("word", i)); } In C++ that is a vector<string>: one heap allocation for the vector, one per string, a pointer and a capacity per element, and a walk over all of them when it goes out of scope. The example you've provided uses strings that are eligible for small string optimization, which avoids per-string allocation. All mainstream STL implementations perform this optimization. You can read about several implementations here: https://devblogs.microsoft.com/oldnewthing/20230803-00/?p=10..."

"I'm curious, for someone more familiar with Goose: how do you represent an arbitrary number of dynamically sized values? If you have a fixed number of stacks, it seems like only a fixed number of values can change size at any given time.For instance, how would you represent a hash table where the values are strings and at random, the program inserts or deletes a random string?This doesn't seem like something you could easily represent. The only way I can think of is to keep a stack that holds all of your strings, and you periodically compact it.Edit: Maybe you could also have a separate stack for strings of various sizes. One each for strings less than 8, 16, ... bytes. But at that point you're basically implementing your own malloc by hand.Edit: Fwiw I do think Goose is very cool! I've had to hand-roll a lot of similar stuff in Rust before to speed up memory management"

"> I compared to a 10-year old CNN and to my surprise found that this was superior.I'm surprised he's surprised.> A small, old, specialised network using an outdated architecture recognises skin lesions far better than two frontier LLMs.Yes of course. A 30m params Katago network with one playout is superhuman in playing go, while frontier LLMs have over 10,000 times more parameters and aren't any good at playing go. They're good at other things (such as helping you create a network that's good at playing go)."

"> I used Inception v3 pretrained on everyday photographs and then fine-tuned all layers on 68,011 dermoscopic and clinical images from the ISIC Archive on a single H100 GPU.>These 200 test lesions, and every other photograph of the same lesion or patient, were removed from its data (including the validation set) before training, so it had never seen them, and they were not used to choose the model or set its threshold.This is just benchmaxxing. A benchmaxxed model will always beat an unbenchmaxxed model, but it will be brittle.You can always beat a specific dataset with a small model by finetuning on a train/test split. I don't understand quantum physics (2018)

"So many entry-level explanations of quantum mechanics start with classical physics, waves, particles, the double-slit experiment, etc. and then jump straight to infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. In my opinion, there's way too much conceptual baggage that comes with these concepts [0].As a non-physicist, QM only clicked for me when I learned the linear algebra of one- and two-qubit systems and observables on those systems [1]. The math is accessible at a pre-calculus level, and the most "counter-intuitive" aspects of QM (interference, the uncertainty principle, entanglement) make perfect sense in this framework. I think it's the best starting point for understanding what QM really says.[0] That's not to say they aren't important historically or for a complete understanding of QM. I'm talking about conceptual understanding for someone, especially an amateur, encountering QM for the first time.[1] Chapter 2 of Nielsen and Chuang is what did it for me. It's heavygoing if you've never seen linear algebra before, but I don't know of a clearer explanation of the postulates of quantum mechanics."

"Of possible interestBasic Concepts Of Quantum Mechanics by L. V. Tarasovhttps://archive.org/details/tarasov-basic-concepts-of-quantu...The dialogues of the author engages with various personas and the reader that are interspersed as Prelude and Interludes are really enlightening.For example:Classical Physicist: Can one seriously speak about an object without having an idea of what it looks like? Isn’t it strange that we study, for example, the behaviour of an electron in a crystal while we don’ t even know what an electron actually is?Author: I don’t agree that we don’t even know what an electron is. I have just indicated a number of precisely determined characteristics and properties of an electron. More detailed properties of microparticles in general and electrons in particular were considered in the preceding sections of the book (and will be considered in the following sections). In fact, we know quite a lot about the electron and know, in particular, about its behaviour in a crystal. This is evidenced by the large number of semiconducting devices fabricated and used by us in practice. As you will see, the absence of a “physically intuitive” model of the electron has in no way turned out to be a serious obstacle. We may even go a step further and state that an understanding of natural phenomena in which Planck’s constant plays an important role is possible only through a significant rejection of a graphic description. By the way, this idea was given by Heisenberg, in whose works much attention was given to questions of the use of physically intuitive methods."
