Inside Deutsche Bank

"I was involved with this project as a reporter, though others from my organisation did most of the work.HN readers may be interested to know a bit about the tech stack. The 15 million or so documents, mostly Outlook mailbox exports, were provided to partners in the consortium via a tool called OpenAleph [1], which is maintained by DARC and based on ElasticSearch and a schema called FollowTheMoney [2], developed specifically for the investigative journalism use case. A lot of the original work on this was done by Friedrich Lindenberg (@pudo), who now runs OpenSanctions [3].The timing was v. interesting given where we are with LLMs -- it's the first major leak I've worked on where it was possible to leverage local models to do some of the heavy lifting, and this only got more possible as the project went on. The coordinators provided a centrally hosted chatbot with RAG, but I also managed to get some surprisingly useful material using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on my 24GB M2 MBA via a custom agent harness for Aleph/OpenAleph. [4]Happy to answer any questions about techy stuff, though I'm not a spokesperson for the project by any means. Seen a few people saying "just release the data!", but I think that slightly misunderstands what this work is. The whole point of forming a consortium of hundreds of journalists and sifting through the dump is to identify the bits that are in the public interest to report on -- especially important in jurisdictions like the UK, where I'm based, which have very strong defamation laws. Think Panama Papers, not Wikileaks.[1] https://darc.li/openaleph/ [2] https://followthemoney.tech/ [3] https://www.opensanctions.org/ [4] https://github.com/data-desk-eco/sift"

"“30+ hours” of free labour in the recruitment process is something YC should put their foot down about, by itself.If that was happening in the UK (or Europe AFAIK) it would be a straightforward breach of employment law.In the UK it’s certainly legal to ask a potential candidate to work an unpaid trial shift as part of the recruitment process (it can be an effective way for some candidates to show they are capable when they don’t do so well in formal interviews) but the rule is simple: the potential employer is not supposed to gain directly from this process. So if someone is supervised or monitored during the shift, for example, the gain from their work is offset by the loss of direct usefulness of the employee supervising.A single “shift” should not even be a full day; that is unlikely to meet the associated reasonableness test. At 12 hours of work from the candidate that would pretty certainly be breaching the law.If a pub asks someone to “work a couple of trial shifts” even that is possibly illegal if they are not paid.Consequently this process is not all that common outside of customer-facing work (retail and hospitality); paid probationary periods are more common.(Given the law here about the employer not deriving a direct gain from the recruitment process itself, it seems implausible that grotesque LinkedIn shit could be legal)ETA: I guess my main point in this under-caffeinated ramble is that YC should be making it clear that, even in a startup context, obviously uncompensated work during the recruitment process is abusive."
