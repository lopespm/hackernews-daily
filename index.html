Inkling-2-Mini in 5.4 GB Memory

"If you're new to quantized models: Don't get too excited about claims of fitting large models into tiny RAM footprints from random GitHub repos. There is always a tradeoff, and it's too easy to benchmaxx KL-divergence numbers and even single benchmark scores. Just because you can get something to run on a device doesn't mean it's going to be useful. The headline claim of this repo (12X better) doesn't even apply to Inkling-2-MiniThis repo's README is a mess of different histories as the author bounces from one popular model to the next. It refers to vague things like "the 1.2GB file" that probably made sense in an early vibecoding pass, but are now lost after the repo has been pushed around to different popular models.The truth is that random GitHub repos with messy READMEs almost never hold a real secret that the mainstream quantizations have missed. I would recommend sticking to mainstream quantizations like the official GGML release, Unsloth's quants, or the more well known community quantizers like bartowski or AesSedai. They typically follow an optimized size-quality tradeoff so you can find the one that fits your needs.When you see a repo like this where everything is so obviously vibecoded that the README is a rambling mix of projects and claims, you should probably go looking for a more authoritative source. The local LLM communities get spammed with 10 of these types of projects per day but I have yet to see a real breakthrough come from any of the vibecoded ones. The real work is coming from people who study this and have compute to test at scale or work on implementing advanced techniques which require inference engine support, like trellis quants."

What Happened to Silicon Valley?

"It’s a long article, and the most important idea is buried towards the end:> That’s where I come back to the idea of mechanism design as reverse game theory. Rather than asking ourselves the rules of the game we are now playing, it calls us to ask “What game ought we to be playing?” And that question leads me imperatively to my answer: “The game of human flourishing.”I wholeheartedly agree: we should think way harder about what we want humanity to optimize for collectively, rather than keeping it haphazard.The current economic system is built on the idea of ‘shareholder value maximisation’, which, in a finite world, will ultimately become simply ‘value extraction’. With AI, we’ve built an automated value extractor, which can extract value even faster than was done before. Because of that, the old model can no longer work for improving human flourishing — in a finite world, it will inevitably lead to economic accumulation by few entities, throttling the economic flow."

"Attempts at governance are futile until we recognize that we have built a machine and set it on its course. Instead, we pretend that the market is a natural phenomenon best left alone, and we fail to hold its mechanism designers to account. We need to tear down and rebuild that machine, reprogramming it so that human flourishing, not corporate profit, becomes its goal.This article has useful insights. It points out that capitalism is an optimization engine for something. Shareholder value and CEO compensation, mostly. It reminds us of something that many people would prefer be forgotten - capitalism operates under rules set by governments, and those shape the market. Tax law matters - what's deductible warps spending patterns. Bankruptcy law matters - who gets left holding the bag? Corporation law matters - how immune are executives from the consequences of their decisions? Labor law matters - do people go to jail for wage and hour law violations?All of those are changeable by legislation. Most have become much more favorable to companies since 1970. That's not necessarily permanent. We had a New Deal in the 1930s, following the Gilded Age of the 1920s and the crash of 1929."

Perplexity and Intelligence

"Perplexity is not defined in the article. FYI:Perplexity is the degree of surprise the model has in predicting the next token, over a sequence of tokens. At the worst case, perplexity is equal to the size of the vocabulary. At the best case, perplexity is 1.So, given a sequence of tokens, "e = m c ^ 2", if the p(token | prev tokens) = 1 for every token, perplexity ends up being 1. If, however, it's a random predictor, the p(token | prev tokens) is 1/vocab for every token, and perplexity ends up being len(vocab).The article plots log(perplexity) on the y-axis, which puts the best case at 0 (i.e. log 1)."

"This trend is pretty shocking to me. I assumed that we had moved beyond cross-entropy loss being important with the increasing use of RL, it's wild that the trend is so clear. Really nice to have such a simple benchmark instead of the increasingly convoluted ones that are getting spun up every day.Also, I wonder what happened with GPT 4.5? "The perplexity / intelligence correspondence is one of those ideas that is surprising at first, but in retrospect could not work any other way. The "magic" of intelligence is the ability to extract sophisticated world models from large volumes of raw sensor data. The prediction (=compression) rate on the raw data stream is exactly the metric that captures this progress.I argued this point in a 2008 paper: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-69134-1_...I elaborated on this idea in my 2011 book: https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.5466I used the word "reusability" to express the idea that the world models obtained via the large scale data compression task could be repurposed for other tasks.(So, Burfoot, if you were so prescient, why aren't you a rich and famous frontier lab researcher? The answer is: I did not think neural nets would scale up)."
