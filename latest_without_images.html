Hard Drive pricing has gotten bad

"Author assumes price cartels, but this can be completely explained by supply and demand. Hyperscalars are buying all the storage they can get their hands on; that's it. Manufacturers are loath to ramp up supply (high CapEx), because they'll be underwater if the AI demand evaporates."

"Memory, both chips and HDs, eventually collapses in price; that’s been the cycle for decades. The boom-and-bust cycle is what has driven the number of manufacturers down. My suspicion is that, in a few years, the price per gig will collapse again. AI is memory-hungry, and PC manufacturers will be pushing self-hosted AI models, which will require lots of memory. I bet we’ll see an increase in density per unit, which will bring down the price per gig. I give it five years at most. At that time, we will be seeing PC's capable of hosting a full AI model for the equivalen of less that $1000 today. It's how tech has worked for decades. There's no reason to think things have changed."

"Yep, at the start of the year I bought a 8-bay UNAS before the HD price increases had fully “set” in my brain. It still sits empty because I can’t afford to put drives in it.I have my hard drive purchase info back a little over a decade and for the past few years (before 2025) I was settling into ~$14/TB. Now it’s _well_ over double that. The only Seagate Exos 20TB (not refurb) on Amazon I can find is $999, ~$50/TB.It’s a hard time to be homelab-er. AMD-specific fork of llama.cpp

"Early in 2025 a bunch of folks realized that AMD made a bunch of cards with mediocre compute but incredibly fast HBM2 memory in their circa 2018 gfx906 datacenter series, so you could get 32GB VRAM capable of incredible 1 TB / s bandwidth for $200 (the closest in today’s cards is north of $3000)There has been a rich little ecosystem of folks optimizing llama.cpp for these odd cards (no tensor cores, no native bf16 etc.) including my little personal repo https://github.com/kinchahoy/llama-of-vega.cpp. Stuff like this focused on more modern RDNA doesn’t help us at all.It’s been interesting to see how much each new frontier model can figure out in terms of optimizing for these GPUs and what they can’t!Fable and Astra will regularly run out of ideas until I point them to someone’s actually thoughtful repo and then they’ll diligently incorporate ideas.The optimization wins are huge (I was initially skeptical that per model kernel optimizations would be worth much). 27B at a pristine Q8 quant goes from base llama.cpp at some 250 pp / 25 tg to 600 pp / 40 tg even before speculative decoding."

"I saw some blurb a while ago that described llama.cpp as a “model zoo”, which is a great description of its utility and generality.In the last couple of days I have been playing with Erik Paperniuk’s M1 port of Inco’s Splash, which has completely transformed the speed of Qwen 3.8 27B and Inkling 2 Mini; it has essentially tripled the speed of the former and more than doubled the speed of the latter.This means the Qwen model is fully viable on an M1 Max, where before I had found it a bit of a waiting game. And it arguably transforms the utility of Inkling 2 Mini; extra speed means it can be left to reason for longer, the downside of needing to do more tool use for search to patch its knowledge is reduced, and it provides a good rough chat model (though this is not its area of greatest strength).I have not yet found anything that can boost Muse Glimmer to the same extent; I would still prefer that.But for me I now find that only the rather slow prefill speed really holds the M1 Max back; I suspect these new custom kernels have made the M5 Pro Mac Mini a really cost-effective option if you want to tinker with local AI."

"I have a r9700 card and I highly recommend the radiance engine. With a single card it processes (prefill) at 2400 tok/s and decodes at 150 tok/s. With a 4-bit model that is intelligently quantised to have a KLD of 0.006 and top-1 of 0.972 I have been working this engine for a month, throwing a lot of agentic and coding work and it has been doing great!
