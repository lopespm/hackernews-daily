Smart home devices should work without the internet

"Bought our home almost 20 years ago, and the former owner (apparently) owned a now-defunct home automation business. The upside was 100% X10/Insteon power and light controls, top alarm panel, environmental controls and so on running off of a wall-mounted "SmartLinc" touch panel. I expanded the system with Axis cameras and a Mac Server running Indigo and SecuritySpy (non-subscription), which enabled remote monitoring and control, but it all operates locally.In a moment of weakness, I bought a D-Link wireless (wifi) water detector for the sump pit (it was on clearance for $30); it relied on a D-Link Home cloud service (free) that was closed down a few years ago.Not wanting to toss it, I repurposed a spare Chromebook 14 that I was going to recycle and turned it into a Alpine monitor for the water detector, which I'm powering from the Chromebook's USB port (if power is lost in a rainstorm, I need to know about it before the sump pit fills up).An immediate issue was that the D-Link firmware tried to "phone home" every 24 hours and would drop from the local network, since the server was kaput, and I had no clue what response it was expecting from the server to keep it alive. Solution; "reboot" the unit by interrupting USB power (with a Pico and MOSFET) every 23 hours. There's no question that home automation should work without the internet."

"HomeAssistant publishes "IoT Class" for all of it's integrations ranging from "Local Push" to "Cloud Polling": https://www.home-assistant.io/blog/2016/02/12/classifying-th...I only use "Local Polling" or better, and highly prefer "Local Push"."

"The new rules I have come to use are:- buy the cheapest hardware that gets the job done- ignore negative reviews that are software related- upon receipt of devices, ask claude for local-first cfw- have fun for a few hours acting as a meat proxy- boom, cheap local-only smart home is doneUse a SBC or nas for home assistant. This process worked for me for switches, a thermostat, an air quality monitor, a thermometer, and a window fan.Would’ve paid ~2.5x for hardware that had the appropriate local-first capability out of the box. Instead this all fit within an existing $20 subscription to an inference provider. DS 4.1 running locally was on track too just more slowly."

E-means: Outperforming k-means in one-tenth the epochs

"I wonder if this could be used anywhere as an alternative to Lloyd's algorithm. I have a hobby project which uses Lloyd's to "relax" an icosohedral equal area mesh on the surface of a sphere [1]. I'm going to try to swap it out with an adaptation of this and see if it's much better or if I can afford more iterations with this method.Edit: tried it, and it’s definitely not better for this use case. e-means wins by needing fewer passes over a huge dataset, but relaxing a mesh on a sphere has no dataset: each Voronoi cell’s centroid can be computed directly from its geometry. Mini-batches just swap that for a noisy sampled estimate of the same thing. It has the same objective and the same optimum, so at best it matches Lloyd. In practice it was much slower and left the grid noisy, and with small sample budgets it even tangled the mesh.[1] https://webgcm.app/explain/grid#nudging-the-cells-into-shape"

"I adapted the method to my IVF index (the code-daemon project) and achieved a 146x speedup on the GPU.That's impressive!"

"Thanks for this. I tried experimenting with e-means in retrieval engine im building, for that I had to port the CPU version from faiss to Rusthttps://crates.io/crates/emeanshttps://pypi.org/project/emeansWe use k-means to build the cells of our vector index, which today runs 5 Lloyd iterations on a sample of the data. So I ran a quick test on 500k LAION embeddings (768 dimensions, 4,096 cells) against our current code:- Current setup of 5 iterations: about 4x faster, with slightly better recall at every probe count (for example 0.9518 vs 0.9473 at 32 probes) and fewer rows scanned per query- Cells came out much more even. AI usage generates a fragile codebase

"I think looking at AI usage for code and development from an engineering perspective is going to be an exercise in frustration. I'm a software engineer for 20+ years and I've pivoted to using AI for my code 100%. I find it invaluable about the only way to stay relevant and run a business nowadays. Happy for anyone to disagree with me, because I don't want to be right for the first time in my life.That said, my experience tells me that enterprise and business coding has rarely ever been about stable or robust codebases. We engineers and a fraction of competent engineering managers and a vanishing small number of directors fought against business interests to ensure quality was priority when it came to code, but it was always an uphill battle and we were oftentimes disciplined for spending too much time on quality and design.A timed cadence of release is anathema to quality code because you can't timebox it.So, AI is just the extension of that. Do I try to ensure my agents design code I would consider to be good? Yes, absolutely! But I've fully outsourced it and I find it's also fully capable of fixing it's own mistakes. I've gone far beyond anything I individually or even with a team was ever capable of with AI assistance, so I believe I just have to apply my experience and skills into management and architecture rather than actual lines of code.Time only will tell how this plays out.Wallaahu aʿlam."

"This reads like a piece of content written a year ago. Things have radically changed since then."AI writes shallow tests" - used to be true, not true anymore since Astra and Fable 5.1 / Opus 5.5."AI writes overly complex ccode" - only if you let it. A "do not overengineer it" or "Pareto, 80% result with 20% of effort" go a long long way."Reliance on AI decreases human understanding" - human understanding does not decrease, it just moves up a level or two. The same way you do not really understand how the Linux kernel works, nor do you know how assembly really works (with probably a bunch of exceptions here given this is the HN crowd!). And then there is hardware and physics too.Understanding code, algorithms and data structures becomes a rarer need than it used to be."AI moves too quickly to maintain a high quality bar" - if you are stuck with the mantra that humans must review the code today, then obviously that statement becomes true. The problem is the assumption, not the observation. The good news about AI progressing fast is that coding AND reviewing get better and better with each release.I know it is tough to let go. But as a software engineer, the sooner you do it, the quicker you will learn to yield a much, much more powerful weapon."

"I'm not sure that "AI generates..." articles are going to age well.My immediate question would be what kind of adversarial reviews and deterministic gates did the author write to capture the dimensions of maintainability (lack of DRY, cyclomatic complexity, unclear naming, poorly architected flows, etc) that they cared about.Don't get me wrong. When I was speeding along at 200 PRs a day (personal projects - I would not have inflicted them on others) I pretty quickly slopped together a big ball of mud, but as I'm re-generating my estate (a) the models are a little better but (b) I'm adding a lot more rigor around verification (correctness, performance, security, operational costs and maintainability). It's not an easy problem to solve, but it feels to me like an engineering endeavor, not speculative rocket science.I fully understand that many AI generated code bases do indeed suffer from more incidents and less maintainability, but I think it's important to distinguish between what is common and what is possible as the fix for one is improving the rigor of your verification whereas the fix for the other would be either accepting crappy code or giving up on LLMs."
